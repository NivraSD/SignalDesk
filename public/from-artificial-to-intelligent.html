A sudden influx of capital enabled rapid gains in AI. But we underinvested in what it takes to capitalize on them — and now those gains are being jeopardized by the ecosystem that built them, and by the people left out of the building.

The Civilizational Bet

Artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence has rapidly become the foundation we are building our future on — the thing the economy, the markets, and even national security are increasingly staking themselves against. It is also one of the most contentious subjects of the moment, and part of why is that the name itself contains two very different things: the artificial, and the intelligence. The gains are real — a genuine, historic leap in capability. But those gains have been concentrated in very few hands, and getting there required manufacturing the conditions to pile an almost unimaginable amount of cash into a handful of companies, at a speed the modern economy has never seen.

Since 2008, a massive wave of capital — primarily driven by responses to the 2008 financial crisis and COVID, and years of quantitative easing — reshaped the ordinary forces of markets. Money grew abundant, cheap, and desperate for somewhere to compound. Then we bet our future on intelligence — a thing that does not behave like a normal commodity, and that upends those forces further still.

Three sides — all dependent

Artificial

The machines, the chips, the capital. What everyone pictures when they hear the word.

Physical

The plants, the power, the grid, the base materials it all runs on. Traditional, unglamorous, essential.

Intelligence

The capability, the judgment, the collective. The actual prize — the reason to build any of it.

These three layers are wholly dependent on one another. And the gaps between them — capital that cannot reach the buildout, a buildout that cannot reach the people who could man it — are now hanging by a thread.

Left unchecked, those gaps do not just mean things go unbuilt. There is a darker turn already underway. We have spent decades gutting our ability to make physical things — the shipyards, the steel, the grid. And now, in the way the AI bet is being made, we are turning people against the one thing that could restore that ability: intelligence itself.

A civilization that has already hollowed out its capacity to build, and then goes after the thing that could rebuild it, ends predictably. That people are growing actively hostile to intelligence — the very thing that could improve their lives — is, when you say it plainly, a little crazy. It is also the clearest sign that the way we are building this poses a threat to the whole.

First · How Real It Already Is

The froth may be a bubble. The capability is not.

Below-average human on major benchmarks in 2020. Called AGI by its makers — Jensen Huang among them — in 2026. Entirely new skills born from nothing. And already the majority of the web. The intelligence is real, and it is here.

The Machines

Compute isn't a commodity. It's a currency.

The clearest sign intelligence has already changed the economy: the economy grew a central bank for it — one it never designed and cannot vote out. NVIDIA does not just sell chips; it issues compute, extending it for equity and claims, the way a central bank issues money against paper.

  • A commodity's supply catches up and its price falls. Compute's cannot — the capability to make it is not for sale.
  • It is worth more than money: you cannot build without it, and money will not buy it if the issuer will not allocate it.
  • Why NVIDIA and not the rivals? A head start, and an ecosystem built with it. You can copy a chip; you cannot copy nineteen years.
The hardest currency in the AI economy is not money. It is the chips.

And when the currency-issuer wants something, price is barely a consideration. Late in 2025, Hugging Face — the open-source hub much of AI is built on — turned down a ~$500M NVIDIA investment, at roughly a $7B valuation, to protect its neutrality. Months later NVIDIA simply bought the whole company for $12,930,300,000 — a figure quoted to the exact dollar, a wink more than a valuation. When a $13B acquisition can be priced like a punchline, the cash was never really the point.

And $13B is itself a rounding error. Weeks earlier, six of the largest financial institutions on earth — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR — lined up to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital around NVIDIA compute. The entire Hugging Face purchase is under 3% of it. Jensen Huang's own words for why compute anchors the money: it is "fungible and transferable" — the language of a currency, not a component.

The Ground

All the money in the world can't buy a data center.

The largest data center project ever proposed in the United States — the ~$100 billion Prince William Digital Gateway in Virginia, developed by QTS (owned by Blackstone) and Compass Datacenters (backed by Brookfield) — was not toppled by lack of money. It was toppled by organized community opposition and a string of court defeats, closing for good in July 2026. A 2,100-acre, city-sized power draw next to the Manassas battlefield required something no balance sheet contains: consent, legitimacy, the social license to build.

Two Worlds

Paper soared while the capacity to build collapsed.

And beneath the fight for consent sits a harder fact: the capacity to build in the real world has been hollowing out for decades, exactly as the paper world inflated. The money multiplied on paper while the power to make physical things drained away — and the AI buildout now needs precisely that physical capacity. The bottleneck is not capital. It is capability — and capability, unlike capital, cannot be printed.

This is not a forecast. It is already visible on the factory floor. US manufacturing has carried 600,000 to 800,000 unfilled jobs every month for four years — the longest sustained labor gap in modern industrial history — with up to 1.9 million roles projected to go unfilled by the early 2030s. And automation makes it worse, not better: as plants add robots to cover the jobs they can't fill, they discover they need more skilled people, not fewer — robotics technicians, automation specialists, the people who program and maintain the machines. If robots are the future of the factory, that future runs directly into a wall of people we have not trained. The shovels are there. Too few hands can pick them up.

The Security of Nations

Why the blank check exists.

None of the speed and scale makes sense until you see what the bet is written against: falling behind. And falling behind, here, is understood as existential — not losing a market, but losing the century. AI sovereignty is now discussed as the new oil; nations speak of spheres of AI influence; chip access has become a diplomatic lever. At the sharpest edge, the UN is pressing a treaty on machines that select their own targets.

When the stakes are framed that way, you do not haggle over the price. You write the blank check. The froth, from this angle, is not only greed. It is fear.

The cruel irony: the blank check, written out of fear of falling behind, is producing the very concentration that could make us fall behind.
The Prize Itself

Intelligence can't be artificial.

We barely understand human intelligence — what it is, how it arises, why it works. So there is something quietly presumptuous in the label we chose. To call a thing artificial is to claim you know what the real version is — and we have just scratched the surface of understanding what any form of intelligence actually is.

Here is the distinction that matters. Intelligence, and the capability to produce and use it, is real — durable, valuable, the actual prize. The artificial parts are interchangeable: the particular chips, the particular company, the particular architecture that happens to hold the lead today. Those are the current form, and the form always changes. The intelligence is what endures — and who knows where or how it will finally live.

Building collective intelligence isn't optional. It is as tangible as roads or literacy — infrastructure for the mind. And infrastructure that only a few can reach is infrastructure that fails.
The Bill, and the Choice

A bill coming due is not a fate. It is a fork.

Costs are already rising. The value of money was cut once this decade. The state's own debt now costs more to service than its defense — the first sustained time in roughly a century. Layer sovereign-scale AI debt on top, and the pressure points one way: more inflation. Should these trends continue — and the count of stalled projects is the gauge — there is little reason to believe we are not at risk of another major inflationary event.

So there is a choice.

Desperation
Turn paper into more paper. Hoard, panic, socialize the losses, chase the froth — and dig the hole deeper.
→ the squeeze tightens
or
Re-couple
Stop turning paper into more paper. Convert it into the collective intelligence that actually propels us — capital and capability, joined again.
→ the squeeze relieves

The squeeze is the product of concentration. What relieves it is distribution — of the capability, not just the capital.

Intelligence isn't artificial.

It will be embedded in how we learn, how we work, how we interact — infrastructure for the mind, as woven into ordinary life as electricity or literacy. But that infrastructure rests on something the money cannot conjure: people capable of building it, maintaining it, enhancing it, and using it to solve our hardest problems.
